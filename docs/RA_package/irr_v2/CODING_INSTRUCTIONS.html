Coding reliability check

Two coders, same items, coded independently · about 90 minutes

You and one other coder each code the same items without seeing our codes or each other's. What we most want is how often two humans agree with each other — that tells us how hard the task really is, and it's the only way to judge whether the automated coding is good enough. Disagreements are the data, not mistakes.

Three rules.
  • Don't look at the project's dataset or any previous coding.
  • Don't discuss items with the other coder until both of you are finished.
  • Unsure? Give your best answer and mark low confidence. A guess beats a blank.

There are two parts, and they're deliberately different. Part 1 is quick classification from short text snippets. Part 2 asks you to open the actual paper — because the two things it tests (the exact value, and the denominator) genuinely need the full paper, which is also what we used.

The idea that governs the whole thing

Everything turns on what a percentage is a share OF — its denominator. Ask: if this number were 100%, what would that mean? The answer tells you both the construct (Part 1) and the denominator (Part 2).

Part 1 — classification (24 items, ~35 min)

Open code_<yourname>.html — it walks you through every item and saves as you go. Each row gives the paper's reported quantity (a sentence) and how that paper defined misinformation. From those two things, fill:

YOUR_construct — what is the % a share of?

all the items in a corpus (posts, videos, articles, results)CONTENT
what people actually consumed (views, visits, clicks, time)EXPOSURE
people who encountered it at least onceREACH
what was shared / retweeted / likedSHARING
people who said in a survey they'd seen itRECALL
activity from a top slice of users/sources ("top 1% → 70%")CONCENTRATION
a quality/usefulness rating, not truthQUALITY
none of theseOTHER

YOUR_breadth — how the paper's definition drew the line (narrowest → widest): fabricated · false · misleading · unreliable_source (judged by the source, not per item) · low_quality. Pick ONE — it's a narrow-to-wide scale, so when a definition spans several ("false or misleading"), code the widest it includes; source-list operationalisations are unreliable_source regardless of wording, and quality ratings are low_quality. If the definition cell is blank (a few papers never state one), code breadth from the wording of the reported quantity itself.

YOUR_confidence_1to5 — 1 = guess, 5 = certain.

Two things that catch people: (1) "most-shared posts, X% false" is CONTENT — sharing was just how the sample was drawn. (2) If a low score is compatible with the content being true, it's QUALITY, not falsity.

Part 2 — read the paper (12 items, ~55 min)

Part 2 continues on the same page. Each row names a paper whose PDF is in the papers/ folder, and a what_to_find pointer. The value has been removed. Open the PDF and, from the paper itself, fill:

  • YOUR_value_pct — the number the pointer describes, as a plain number (12.4, not 12.4%). Report the single overall figure, not a subgroup or per-country split.
  • YOUR_what_it_is_a_share_of — in your own words, the denominator. This is the crux.
  • YOUR_denominator_class — pick one: population (people) · all_media (whole media diet, incl. non-news) · news_diet (all news, any topic) · political_news (politics only) · topical (one issue, keyword search) · curated_sample (hand-picked or top-N) · single_source (one account/outlet). Leave blank for CONCENTRATION and OTHER — it doesn't apply.
  • YOUR_construct (re-confirm) and YOUR_confidence_1to5.

The denominator often isn't in the results sentence — you'll usually have to check the methods (how the sample was built). That's why Part 2 gives you the whole paper. Can't find the number in ~5 min? Write NOT FOUND; that's a finding about the paper, not a failure.
