You are helping code data for a systematic review of how much misinformation people are exposed to. For each numbered item you are given a verbatim quote from a research paper and how that paper defined misinformation.

Classify each item on three dimensions. The governing rule: THE CONSTRUCT IS SET BY THE DENOMINATOR -- by what the percentage is a share OF -- not by the topic and not by what is being counted on top. Ask yourself: if this percentage were 100%, what would that mean? The answer names the denominator.

CONSTRUCT (choose exactly one):
- CONTENT = share of items that exist in some corpus (posts, videos, articles, search results)
- EXPOSURE = share of what people actually consumed (views, visits, clicks, time, news diet)
- REACH = share of PEOPLE who encountered it at least once
- SHARING = share of what was shared, retweeted, liked or engaged with
- RECALL = share of people who SAID in a survey they had seen it
- CONCENTRATION = activity attributable to a top slice of users or sources
- QUALITY = a quality/usefulness rating rather than truth or falsity
- OTHER = none of the above

DENOMINATOR CLASS (choose exactly one):
population | all_media | political_news | topical | curated_sample | single_source

BREADTH -- how wide a net the paper cast in deciding what counts as misinformation:
fabricated | false | misleading | unreliable_source | low_quality

RULES THAT DECIDE THE HARD CASES:

1. "Most-shared" as a SAMPLING METHOD is still CONTENT. If a paper collected the 500 most-shared posts and then coded how many are false, the denominator is those 500 POSTS. Code CONTENT. Sharing was only how the sample was drawn. Contrast: "3% of all links shared by our panel were fake-news links" -- there the denominator really is the shared links, so SHARING.

2. Quality is not falsity. If the measure is about usefulness, completeness, reliability or guideline-adherence rather than truth, code QUALITY. Test: could the item be ACCURATE BUT UNHELPFUL? If a low score is compatible with the content being true, it is QUALITY. Note that a single paper often reports BOTH a quality score and a separate falsity measure -- judge the quote in front of you, not the paper as a whole.

3. Visiting untrustworthy websites is EXPOSURE. Identifying misinformation by DOMAIN rather than by checking each article does not change the denominator; the denominator is still the person's browsing. Record that in breadth as unreliable_source.

4. SURVEYS: the denominator is the PEOPLE ASKED, not the topic. "38% of respondents said they had seen false claims about COVID" is denominator_class = population. It is NOT topical just because the question named a topic. If it were 100%, it would mean every person surveyed said yes -- so the denominator is people. The same applies to "% of people who visited at least one unreliable site" (REACH): the denominator is the people observed, however narrow the thing being counted is.

5. TWO KINDS OF "EVERYTHING" DENOMINATOR. Distinguish a person's WHOLE MEDIA diet from their NEWS diet -- they differ roughly twentyfold on the same phenomenon. "0.15% of total daily media consumption across TV, desktop and mobile" is all_media. "3.4% of all news web traffic" is a news-wide denominator: use all_media ONLY if the denominator includes non-news media too; if it is all news but NOT restricted to politics, choose the closest fit and say which in a trailing note. Use political_news only when the denominator is restricted to POLITICAL or ELECTION news.

6. Denominators that are already misinformation. "Of the false claims we collected, 30% were about vaccines" is not a prevalence at all -- the denominator is the false items themselves. Code OTHER.

Answer with ONE CSV line per item and NOTHING else -- no preamble, no commentary, no sources.
Format: item_id,construct,denominator_class,breadth,confidence_1to5
Use confidence 1-5 honestly; a 2 is genuinely useful information. If you cannot judge an item, output the item_id followed by UNSURE and nothing else for that row -- do not guess silently.
